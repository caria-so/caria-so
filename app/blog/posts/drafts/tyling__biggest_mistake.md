# Introduction  
I just found this text in my archive dating back to 2017 while doing some cleaning in my computer. The text was the subject of a talk at Cologne University, dating back to 2017.

Mi pare ieri, che la scrivevo nei bar di Palermo, Buenos Aires, doveva essere la mia best talk, dopo una boccata di aria fresca fuori dall'Europa, molte ricerche con lo scopo di preparae un post-doc. 

A Colonia nessuno mi voleva veramente bene,

Tutti avevano avuto la bozza finale per tempo, c' è da dire che non serviva neanche quella poiche tutti erano parte del Marie Curie Network e avevamo lavorato insieme per 4 anni, fatto convegni, presentato e ripresentato le stesse cose, la mia tesi la sapevano a memoria. 

Allora ho pensato di   try to give the team a glimpse of direction my postdoc might take. The title was a program, it was called **As Books might Think**. The result? A complete disaster 💥😂. 
Here are some paragraphs:


> As Books Might Think

> 黙殺

> First-generation AI-powered tools will be getting out of the labs sometime quite soon. These enhancements come from recent breakthroughs in deep learning techniques, which promise to push the field of AI so far in the next ten years that every prediction about the future will have a kind of post-human flavor.

> An Attendant Robot 

> Despite governments and companies stepping forward to the next world war, I believe there is still a lot to do before such subspecies of killers get smart enough to stitch a bullet in your back while you're in your home's toilet.

> Building intelligent artificial agents mainly relies on figuring out what intelligence is, and nobody knows that yet, apart from those who are convinced that language is intelligence. Now, it's really tempting to hesitate on this funny landscape but more important for us is to keep brainstorming the near future in order to figure out the ROI that we, as academics who have been involved in this field practically forever might enjoy. In fact, all this buzz really plays to our advantage. Language research will soon be the bleeding edge! Hard to believe. One day we were in the basement of a dusty crappy department, the next day we find ourselves among the highest paid researchers in industry and government. More and more, governments, big companies, and entrepreneurs will be putting computer scientists to work on natural language. More on this sci-fi in the next chapter. Forget Siri silliness, forget IBM's Watson, Parsey McParseface, and all this prehistory. It's changing, here we're talking about something huge:

> - **Keyword search will be replaced** together with the unnameable SEO by something called **"prompts"**. Now imagine a bash terminal. Prompts are essentially bash commands written in natural language - a bit like texting your partner a grocery list - that are interpreted by the machine as if they were bash gibberish. People will interact with computers via those commands. The enthusiasts are already shouting that this is the end of the GUI but we'll have to wait and see how that evolves.

> - We might move towards **a fuzzier idea of computer output**—evolution took the design risks that Von Neumann didn't! Embracing this change might need computers to take risks just like biological systems do, in order to enable decentralized control, parallel processing, overlaid outputs, large amounts of raw data. Contrary to computers, the brain is self-initiated (e.g., feedback loops) and has the ability to learn from experience.

> - Natural language understanding and generation might go beyond statistical methods or require statistical capabilities **we are not equipped for**. If all this stuff is going to happen, it must happen somewhere outside of CPUs, and if I am right, a whole new market is going to open up for finding alternatives to CPU shortages.

> 1.2. "Would Rather Like To Be A Philosophy Major"

> Some believes critical thinking will become yet more valuable in 10 years, as computers and robots increasingly replace human technical skills. The theme of 800 million global workers who will lose their jobs by 2030 and be replaced by robotic automation seems to be another trendy argument on the Web right in summer 2017. We have to see that.

> In a way, this is nothing new, since technology has been replacing human labor since the invention of the wheel, but this is going to happen little by little — it is already happening — starting from the less critical tasks. Most likely the earliest outcome of recent experiments will hit the market with considerable caution, just tackling relatively low-level skill, low-wage, highly repetitive and ultra-boring work.

> As for most people in high-skill positions, instead of being replaced wholesale, they will more likely find themselves working alongside their **synthetic colleagues**. 60% of today's occupations have at least some portion that can be automated, and human-assisted training of these systems produces more accurate results while training more robust systems going forward. This is the scenario we should expect for the next few years, before leveling up the game. We might try to forecast how that would look from the early experiments: 

> Facebook is testing **Project M** within its Messenger App to allow users to issue commands, access services, and make purchases through text input. 

> A sort of command line that figures out plain text. The most interesting thing about M is the concept of "humans in the loop": this is key for us too, not only to imagine a good case of human-computer early symbiosis - here the bargain is all about human-generated tokens versus computer performance - but to forecast a game that has a sort of two levels, where the next one is machines starting to want neurobiological data.

> As books might think, Summer 2017


The rest is messed up and I don't have time to fix it; plus it doesn't interest anyone since it concerns xml/tei and the completely opposite direction to this future that we were so keen on pursuing.

#### The day after
Now that I stop to think about it I remember the grimaces of disgust that gradually formed on the faces of the listeners. People horrified, they had no questions, no comment. I just got an email the day after from one of the guys in the commission who told me I was a charlatan, and that "he regretted not going on vacation that day as he had planned". I was so mortified that I abandoned these ideas, left academia, stopped following it, found another job.

### Takeaway
Sure a commission of galactic-mega-professors who wrinkle their noses in unison (and not just as I explained) can be quite capable of convincing you that you're just talking bullshit. And yet in my heart I feel that I was weak to listen to them. This was one of my biggest professional mistakes, not pushing everything into this topic in 2017. Now I wouldn't find myself pulling all-nighters to keep up.
