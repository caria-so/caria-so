Background

Knowledge Shorts Twice: Once in the tools.
Once in the humans.

A researcher navigating a corpus of 10,000 papers and a shopper filtering a product catalog are dealing with the same problem — moving through complex information under uncertainty to find what they need. The interfaces differ. The cognitive architecture doesn't.

I started on the hard version: scientific publishing tools, critical apparatuses, information-seeking and research behavior in academic contexts. Marie Curie fellowship, PhD in HCI, three first-author papers on why knowledge tools fail their users. Then eight years at Baymard Institute applying the same evaluation discipline to e-commerce — 700+ usability heuristics, Fortune 500 scale. The finding was always the same: tools assuming a user who doesn't exist.

The underlying cognitive primitives — recognition over recall, progressive disclosure, feedback loops, spatial memory — are the same whether the interface is a checkout flow, a command line, or a graph database explorer. The skill is seeing that. The advantage is also building it: no handoff, no translation loss, no gap between what the research found and what the code does.
